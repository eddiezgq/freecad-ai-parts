# Housings: casting and machining

## Casting

At casting inspection each piece records:
- Brinell hardness (180–240 HB);
- Tensile strength of the separately cast test bar from this heat (≥ 250 MPa);
- Critical wall thickness;
- Gas holes and sand holes at critical locations (must be 0);
- Moisture of the molding sand when this mold was poured (3.0–3.8 %).

Two common defects:

- **High sand moisture → gas holes**. When the sand mixer's water control fails, water in the sand turns to gas during pouring and stays in the casting. Gas holes often hide under the skin and only show after machining, so casting inspection looks at the bearing bosses.
- **Insufficient inoculation → high hardness, chipped tools in machining**. When the inoculant is damp, or pouring is delayed too long after inoculation (fading), graphite does not form well, the casting is hard, and the chill wedge's white iron zone widens.

## Rules for housing machining (textbook 25.4)

1. The first operation uses the cast holes of the upper and lower shaft bores as the **rough datum** (tapered pins in the holes find the center), ensuring even stock on the most important bores; a rough datum is used only once.
2. From then on locate with **one face and two pins**: base face A + two tooling pin holes (unified datum).
3. **Separate roughing and finishing**, with a **second aging** after roughing to relieve again the stresses released by machining.
4. Bores on one axis are **bored through in one pass** with a long boring bar supported by guide bushings on the fixture, without turning the part around; before boring, probe the top face of the base plate and correct the bore coordinates (the operation dimension conversion in textbook 25.4.6).
5. Deburr intersecting holes and measure cleanliness after washing; overlock housings get an oil sump leak test.
6. Topcoat only after all machining is done and final inspection passed: mask the holes and machined faces first, then bake at low temperature.

## What housing final inspection records

| Lockstitch housing | Requirement (example) |
|---|---|
| Upper shaft column bore Ø26 J7 | 25.991–26.012 mm |
| Coaxiality of the three upper shaft bores, of the three lower shaft bores | ⌀0.010 |
| Parallelism of upper and lower shaft bores | ≤ 0.015 |
| Upper-to-lower bore center distance deviation | ± 0.020 |
| Needle bar bore perpendicularity to the upper shaft bore | 0.010 |
| Needle bar bore position relative to the lower shaft bore | ⌀0.030 |
| Lower shaft bore axis to the top face of the base plate | 32 ± 0.03 |
| Flatness of base face A | ≤ 0.020 |
| Cleanliness | ≤ 15 mg |

Every measurement record carries “which HMC did the finish boring”, so you can stratify by HMC.

## Two housing defects

- **Worn locating pin on one HMC**: only housings bored on that machine slowly grow in needle bar bore position and center distance; the other HMCs are normal. Rebore saves only one piece; replace that fixture's locating pins and bushings.
- **Aging furnace thermocouple drift**: the gauge shows the temperature is reached but the real furnace temperature is low, residual stress is not removed, and housing coaxiality and flatness worsen. It may still pass at shipment, but after a few months with the user the arm slowly deforms and the hook point gap changes (textbook 25.1, problem one).
